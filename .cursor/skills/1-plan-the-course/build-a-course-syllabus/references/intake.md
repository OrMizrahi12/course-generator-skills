## The six questions

Ask these, adapted to what the conversation already told you. Six is the
ceiling, not the target.

1. **Subject** — which product, feature area, or job is the course about?
   Without this every later answer is guesswork.
2. **Size** — how many lessons, or how long should the whole course feel?
   Drives how coarse each lesson is allowed to be.
3. **Audience and prior knowledge** — who watches, and what can they already
   do? Decides where lesson 1 starts and what never needs explaining.
4. **Target level** — first hour, daily user, or power user? Decides the
   ceiling of the last lesson.
5. **Delivery character** — silent screen lessons, live practice, product
   walkthrough? Changes what a lesson can contain.
6. **In and out** — anything that must be covered, and anything deliberately
   left out? The out-of-scope list starts here.

## What you may decide alone

Decide these yourself and state the decision in one line so the user can
correct it:

- Lesson order and dependency chain
- Lesson titles and phrasing
- Which live sources to trust
- The shape of the real work each lesson is built around
- The course slug, derived from the title in kebab-case
- Where a lesson has to be split because it cannot be filmed in one take

## What you may never decide alone

Ask. Silence here is how a course ends up being about the wrong thing:

- The subject, or a narrowing of the subject
- The number of lessons, when the user has an opinion about size
- The audience and their prior knowledge
- What must be covered
- Anything the user pays for, installs, or exposes to make a lesson possible

## How to ask

- One short block, numbered, so the user can answer inline.
- The language the user is writing in. The artifact stays English.
- Never re-ask something already answered in the conversation; fill only the
  gaps and say what you already have.
- No preamble about why you are asking. Ask.

## When the user says "just do what you think"

Do not treat that as permission to skip the answers. Convert every gap into a
stated default and get one confirmation:

> Working assumptions: 6 lessons, silent screen, for a developer who has used
> the product for a week, reaching daily-user level. Out: billing and
> enterprise admin. Say the word if any of that is wrong.

Then proceed. The defaults are now on the record and correctable, which is the
point.

## When the subject is too big for a course

"Teach me Cursor" and "teach me Git" are product categories, not courses. Do not
silently narrow them into whatever you find easiest to film.

Offer two or three honest narrowings, each with the job it serves, and let the
user pick:

> "Cursor" is a few courses. Which one: (a) daily agent workflow on a real repo,
> (b) customizing Cursor with rules, skills, and subagents, (c) reviewing and
> shipping agent-written code?

If the user insists on the wide subject, say plainly that the spine will be
shallow at that width, propose the narrowing you would pick, and follow their
call.
